## 1. Cold open  `0:00–0:52`

Open the settings on almost any phone, and you can find a list like this one.

Music, about twelve dollars a month. Streaming, fifteen. Extra cloud storage, three. A fitness app, thirteen. A productivity app, ten. And an AI assistant, twenty.

None of them feels expensive on its own. Together, that is about seventy-three dollars a month, or roughly eight hundred and eighty dollars a year.

This bundle is a made-up example, not anyone's real bill. But the shift behind it is real.

In January 2022, about 2,000 new subscription apps launched in a single month. By January 2026, it was more than 14,700 a month.

So why does every app suddenly want a monthly payment? And who actually comes out ahead?

## 2. When you owned your software  `0:52–1:51`

Not long ago, buying software looked a lot like buying a book.

You paid once, you got a box with a disc, or a download code, and that version was yours to keep using for as long as your computer could run it.

When a new version came out, you decided whether the new features were worth paying for again.

That was never the whole story. Businesses often paid yearly fees for support and updates. Oracle, for example, says that substantially all of its customers buy and renew their software support contracts every year.

But for many everyday products, one purchase was the deal. Then, in May 2013, one of the best-known software makers changed the rules.

Adobe announced that new features for its creative tools would go only to Creative Cloud subscribers, and that Creative Suite 6 would be the last major update for people who had bought perpetual licenses.

## 3. Adobe's switch  `1:51–2:49`

In the short run, the switch hurt.

In fiscal 2012, subscriptions were 15 percent of Adobe's revenue. A year later they were 28 percent, and total revenue fell about 8 percent, from $4.4 billion to $4.06 billion.

Net income fell from $833 million to $290 million.

Why accept that? Because a box is sold once, but a subscription is billed again and again. Revenue that used to arrive in one burst is spread across months and years.

By fiscal 2014, subscriptions were half of Adobe's revenue. In fiscal 2025, they were 96 percent of $23.8 billion.

Not all of that growth came from the subscription switch alone. But almost all of Adobe's revenue now comes from customers who keep paying.

## 4. Two companies  `2:49–4:03`

To see why, imagine two small software companies. Both are hypothetical.

The first sells a photo editor for $120, once. The second sells a similar app for $10 a month.

In month one, the first company looks like the winner. A thousand customers bring in $120,000 at once. The second company collects $10,000.

But next month, the first company has to find a thousand new buyers. The second starts the month with the customers it already has.

If all of them stay for a year, the second company catches up. And it knows, roughly, what next month will bring.

That is the attraction: predictability. Recurring revenue is easier to forecast and to plan around, and it can pay for work that never really ends: fixing bugs, supporting new phones, running servers.

Some products also cost money every time you use them. Files have to be stored somewhere. An AI answer has to be computed. A single payment does not cover a cost that keeps coming back.

But notice the catch in the second company's plan. It only works if those customers stay.

## 5. Subscription math  `4:03–5:37`

Subscription businesses live by a handful of numbers, and they all connect.

Monthly recurring revenue is what the company expects to bill each month from its active subscribers.

Churn is the share of subscribers who leave in a given period. Retention is the opposite: the share who are still paying.

Customer acquisition cost is what the company spends, on ads and promotion, to win one paying customer. And lifetime value is what that customer is worth over all the months they keep paying.

Let's put hypothetical numbers on our ten-dollar app. If it sells through Apple's App Store, Apple's terms give the developer 70 percent of each payment during a subscriber's first year, and 85 percent after that. Developers in Apple's Small Business Program get 85 percent from the start.

So the developer keeps about $7 a month. If 10 percent of subscribers cancel every month, the average subscriber stays about ten months, and brings in roughly $70.

If winning each subscriber costs $40 in advertising, that looks like a healthy business.

But if churn doubles to 20 percent a month, the average subscriber brings in about $35. That is less than it cost to win them. Same app, same price, and now every new customer loses money.

That is why the people who run subscription apps watch churn more closely than almost anything else.

## 6. What the data shows  `5:37–7:02`

So how do real subscription apps actually do? One of the largest data sets comes from RevenueCat, a company that sells the software many apps use to manage their subscriptions.

Its 2026 report covers more than 115,000 apps and more than $16 billion in revenue. It is not every app in the world, only apps that use RevenueCat's tools. But the picture is striking.

One year after launch, the median app earned about $72 a month. The top 10 percent earned more than $2,500 a month.

Only 4.6 percent of newly launched apps reached $10,000 in monthly revenue within two years. And apps launched before 2020 still generate 69 percent of all subscription revenue in the data.

Keeping subscribers is even harder. For subscriptions that started in 2024, the median share still active a year later was about 8 percent on monthly plans, and 28 percent on yearly plans.

Nearly half of monthly subscribers do not renew even once.

Those figures count subscriptions, not people, and payments, not use. Someone can cancel and keep using a free version. Someone else can keep paying and barely open the app.

## 7. Your wallet  `7:02–8:22`

Now look at it from the customer's side.

A household's monthly budget is finite. Every new subscription has to find room in it, often by pushing something else out.

So apps are not only competing with similar apps. A meditation app competes with a streaming service, and an AI assistant competes with extra cloud storage, for the same few dollars each month.

That is why so much effort goes into the first moments: free trials, discounted first months, cheaper yearly plans. Each is a way to get onto the list, and to survive the first renewal.

RevenueCat found that apps asking for payment upfront, behind a hard paywall, turned about five times as many downloads into paying customers as freemium apps did: 10.7 percent versus 2.1 percent within 35 days. Yet a year later, retention was nearly the same.

Meanwhile, small payments add up quietly. Our hypothetical bundle costs less than three dollars a day. Over a year, it comes to nearly nine hundred.

Not every app takes the pure subscription road. In RevenueCat's data, nearly a quarter of the apps pair their subscriptions with a one-time lifetime purchase.

## 8. Where it breaks  `8:22–10:00`

Recurring revenue sounds safe. It is not guaranteed, and it is not the same as profit.

Payments fail. Cards expire. Banks decline charges. In RevenueCat's data, involuntary billing failures caused about 31 percent of subscription cancellations on Google Play, compared with 14 percent on the App Store.

Hype fades. AI-powered apps in the report earned 41 percent more revenue per payer, but their subscribers churned about 30 percent faster.

Some companies try a shortcut: make it easy to sign up and hard to leave. That can backfire. In September 2025, Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion to settle Federal Trade Commission claims that it enrolled customers in Prime without their consent and made cancelling difficult.

And revenue is not profit. Spotify, a streaming service rather than a typical app, ended 2025 with 290 million premium subscribers and €17.2 billion in revenue.

Most of that money goes straight back out, mainly as royalties and distribution costs. Its gross margin was 32 percent, and as recently as 2023 it reported an operating loss of €446 million.

In 2025, its operating income was €2.2 billion. Subscriptions can become very profitable, but only when the costs behind them are under control.

## 9. The real reason  `10:00–11:08`

So why does every app want a subscription?

Because a subscription turns a single sale into a relationship: one that pays every month, can be forecast, and can fund the work of keeping a product alive.

For customers, that can be a good deal. You pay less upfront, you get updates and new features, and you can leave when the product stops being worth it.

It can also be a worse deal. If you use a tool rarely, or would happily keep an older version forever, owning it outright can cost far less. That is why one-time purchases have not disappeared. Even Adobe still licenses perpetual versions of some products.

The data points to something easy to miss. Most subscriptions do not last. Most apps never make much money. The few that win are the ones people decide, month after month, are still worth paying for.

Companies would like that payment to be automatic. The best ones still have to earn it, every single month.
